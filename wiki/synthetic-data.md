# Synthetic data

Using a language model to produce training data for a language model. In
[lecture 13](13-data-sources-datasets.md) this appears in three separate places,
and the three are worth keeping distinct because they raise different questions.

## 1. As a filtering alternative: Nemotron-CC

The lecture's main treatment. Nemotron-CC's premise is that the field
[over-filters](data-filtering.md#the-counter-argument-everyone-over-filters) —
DCLM and FineWebEdu discard ~90% of the data — and that frontier models need more
tokens than that leaves. Synthetic rewriting is its answer to *what to do with the
data you would otherwise throw away* (≈1:08:35):

- **For low-quality data:** use a language model to **rephrase** it, "to make it
  look more like Wikipedia."
- **For high-quality data:** use a language model to **generate tasks** from it —
  QA pairs, summaries, key-information extraction from a Wikipedia article.

This is a genuinely different move from every other entry in the dataset
chronology. Every other pipeline decides which documents to *keep*; this one
changes documents so they can be kept. The lecture calls Nemotron-CC "probably... one
of the main datasets that really lean into synthetic data for pre-training"
(≈1:08:35).

Result: **6.3T tokens** against DCLM's 3.8T.

![Table of Nemotron-CC results against previous datasets](../raw/images/13-data-sources-datasets/nemotron-results.png)

*Nemotron-CC's comparison — a **table**, despite the `-results` filename.
[Source](https://github.com/stanford-cs336/lectures/blob/main/images/nemotron-results.png)*

Note also that Nemotron-CC's *classifier* is partly synthetic in origin: they
prompt Nemotron-340B-instruct to score FineWeb documents for educational value and
distil those labels into a fast model (≈1:07:50). The labels, not just the text,
come from a model.

## 2. In the training pipeline generally

The lecture's opening overview places synthetic data in the middle of the pipeline
rather than at the start: mid-training is "higher-quality web data, instruction
data, a bunch of synthetic data often goes here," and post-training brings "more
math and reasoning and coding" (≈3:57). DCLM's positive class is itself GPT-4
output — OpenHermes-2.5 is "essentially instruction data that was generated by
GPT-4" (≈1:05:33) — so model-generated text is already load-bearing even in
pipelines that never call themselves synthetic.

## 3. As a licensing problem

This is where the lecture is most interesting, and it comes from two directions
that do not agree.

A student asks whether there are restrictions on training on model-generated data.
The immediate answer is "the short answer is probably yes," i.e. probably allowed
(≈43:06).

But [CommonPile](data-licensing-and-consent.md#commonpile) — the project trying to
build a corpus from permissively licensed data only — **declined to use synthetic
data at all**, and the reasoning is worth reading in full (≈1:17:55):

> ...training language models on synthetic data from unlicensed sources is
> unclear — is it probably fine, because technically these are open-weight models
> with an MIT license, so you can do whatever you want, use them however you want?
> But these language models were presumably also trained on unlicensed data, so
> it's a little bit of data laundering, if you're really honest about it.

The tension is exact. The *model's* license permits the output. But the model was
trained on text whose licensing is unresolved, so generating from it does not
resolve anything — it launders provenance. If you take the risk-averse position
seriously, synthetic data is not a way around the licensing problem, because the
uncertainty is upstream of the generation.

The lecture does not resolve this. It reports the permissive reading as the likely
legal answer and CommonPile's refusal as the consistent-if-you-mean-it answer, and
leaves both standing.

## 4. In post-training, where it is now the default

[Lecture 14](14-data-filtering-dedup-mixing.md) extends this to the other side of
training, and the framing is stronger than anything in lecture 13: "implicitly, at least in the open community, **almost all the post-training data — most of it — is synthetically generated**" (≈1:14:03). A human teacher is possible but "slow and costs a lot of
money", and even at the frontier the practice is now hybrid human-AI.

Two findings there are worth carrying back to this page, because they complicate
the naive picture of distillation:

- **A better model is not necessarily a better teacher.** OpenThoughts found
  QwQ-32B — "a very old and small model" — outperformed DeepSeek-R1 as a teacher
  (≈1:16:20).
- **Answer filtering did not help.** The obvious quality-control step on synthetic
  responses failed to pay off in that study.

[Lecture 15](15-mid-post-training.md) supplies the evidence behind that framing,
and it is stronger than an assertion. Hugging Face set out to build **Zephyr**
with no distillation at all, went to the same annotation vendors the frontier labs
use, and found the human data "extremely time-consuming, costly, and the results
they were getting were not actually better than using model-based annotations"
(≈1:01:39). They switched to AI feedback. UltraChat and UltraFeedback are now
standard, and Tulu 3 uses model-based annotation throughout its pipeline.

The limit is equally sharp: this is a **catch-up** technology. "If you want to
push the frontier out, you don't necessarily get to play these games" (≈1:02:27),
and world knowledge from lawyers or scientists still has to come from lawyers or
scientists. See [model-based annotation](model-based-annotation.md).

## What is not covered here

This lecture is about pre-training data, so it treats synthetic data only as a
corpus-construction technique. Lecture 14 continues with post-training data
(≈1:21:47), where synthetic generation plays a much larger role, and is not yet
covered in this knowledge base.

Two related questions this lecture raises but does not answer:

- **Does synthetic data help, and by how much?** The lecture reports Nemotron-CC's
  claim that its high-quality subset beats previous datasets (≈1:09:20), but does
  not examine model collapse, diversity loss, or the limits of rephrasing.
- **How much of a published token count is synthetic?** Related to the lecture's
  general warning that headline token counts are hard to interpret, since epochs
  inflate them (≈1:09:20). See [data repetition](data-repetition.md).
